#### Background
- The Framework Act on Water Management states the need for, and support of, fostering water culture, but **it does not define what water culture is.** As a result, each institution and policy interpreted it differently.
- Water management once aimed at flood control and water supply; more recently, there is a stronger trend to also consider the social, artistic, and experiential value of water.
- Abroad, starting with Spain's New Water Culture movement in 1995, the EU, UNESCO, and others have emphasized establishing a water culture grounded in the multidimensional value of water.

#### Research Questions
- What elements make up a Korean water culture, and how can it be defined?
- How well do citizens know water culture, and which values of water do they consider important?
- What should K-water Operation & Management Co., which operates water culture hub facilities, do, and in what order?

#### Data & Methods
- I analyzed domestic and international literature to derive the pillars of water culture and define the concept.
- I designed a **nationwide online survey of 400 people** (2024.12.9–16) on awareness of water culture, the value of water, use of water culture centers, and attitudes toward protecting water, and ran frequency and cross-tabulation analyses.
- Drawing on international cases such as the OECD, I mapped domestic water culture stakeholders.
- I conducted field surveys at the Chungju Dam and Seomjingang Dam water culture centers and the Geumgang River culture center, and reviewed domestic and international cases of spreading water culture.
- Combining these results, I carried out a SWOT analysis and developed a vision, strategies, and an institutional roadmap.

#### Key Findings
- I defined water culture along **four pillars: history and tradition, society and economy, environment and ecology, and arts and leisure**. All values and ways of life created through the interaction of water and people from these four perspectives count as water culture.

- Only 77 of 400 respondents (**19.3%**) had heard the term "water culture." They mostly encountered it on the internet (39.0%), TV and news media (28.6%), and social media (19.5%).
- By contrast, **92.5%** said efforts to preserve and develop water culture are important. This revealed a gap: the term is unfamiliar, but people share its values.
- As important values of water (multiple responses), respondents chose environment and ecology (97.5%) and society and economy (83.5%), while history and tradition (9.0%) and leisure and arts (10.0%) were low. Respondents in their 30s chose leisure and arts relatively more often, at 17.8%.

- The SWOT analysis identified rich water heritage and stable water management infrastructure as strengths, and low public awareness, the lack of a control tower, and a shortage of specialists as weaknesses.
- I set the vision as **"A sustainable water-friendly nation built on water culture"** and proposed 3 goals (raising water awareness and managing demand, fostering a rich water culture, and sustainable water use) and 4 strategies (space management, stronger networks, content development, and industry development).

#### Implications
- Public campaigns and promotion (30.0%) and stronger government policy (26.7%) were the most frequently chosen means of spreading water culture. The public sector needs to take an active role in civic education and promotion.
- Water culture centers and river culture centers are key hubs of water culture but lacked networking, specialists, and educational programs. They need to **become cultural hubs** connected with local communities.
- To broaden perceptions that lean toward environment and socio-economics, content on history and tradition and on arts and leisure should be planned as well.

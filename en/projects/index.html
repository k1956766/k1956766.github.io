Master's Thesis 1

Employment and Income Characteristics of Out-Migrants from Depopulating Areas

Master's Thesis · Graduate School of Environmental Studies, Seoul National University · 2025

Type Master's thesis (M.S. in City Planning)
Period 2024.12 – 2025.7 (degree conferred 2025.8)
Role Sole author, from research design through data construction, analysis, and writing
Data Statistics Korea Population Statistics Register integrated analysis DB (2015–2023, full-population panel of 533,798 people)
Methods Logistic regression, multiple regression, CEM matching + staggered DID
Recognition Best Paper Award, Korean Planning Association Spring Conference (2025.4)

Background

  • Korea’s 89 depopulating si-gun-gu are seeing their local economies weaken under continuous out-migration. From 2020 to 2023, the top reason for moving out of depopulating areas was “work” (28.0%), unlike the national trend, where housing ranks first.
  • Yet prior studies stopped at typologies based on demographic characteristics, such as classifying or clustering areas by risk of regional extinction. Data linking income and employment were hard to obtain.
  • As a result, who leaves, and whether their income actually rises after leaving, had never been tested empirically.

Research Questions

  • What characteristics did people who left depopulating areas have?
  • What factors most affected the income change of out-migrants?
  • How does the rate of income change evolve over time after migration?

Data & Methods

  • Using the Statistics Korea Population Statistics Register integrated analysis DB, I built a full-population panel linking population, housing, employment, and income. I tracked people who lived in depopulating areas in 2015, aged 26 (in 2015) to 64 (in 2023), through 2023, and analyzed 533,798 people. I cleaned about 450 million raw records and automated the analysis modules.
  • Income was defined as the larger of earned and business income, deflated to real terms with each province’s consumer price index.
  • Destinations were classified into 6 types: the Seoul metropolitan area, non-capital large cities, non-capital small and medium cities, other depopulating areas, innovation cities, and non-movers. For people who moved more than once, I treated the area where they lived longest as their effective destination.
  • I used binary and multinomial logistic regression to analyze who leaves, and multiple regression with the 2015→2023 income change rate as the dependent variable to analyze what changes income.
  • Because migration timing differs across individuals, I used the staggered difference-in-differences (staggered DID) method of Callaway & Sant’Anna (2021). I reduced selection bias with CEM matching on age, sex, and income in the 2 years before moving (8 quantiles); after matching, SMDs were close to 0.
  • I estimated rigorously with a balanced panel observed from 2 years before to 3 years after the move (moves in 2018–2020), and supplemented the long-term trend up to 6 years after the move with an unbalanced panel.

Key Findings

  • Those who leave are not the vulnerable but people with strong labor-market competitiveness. Higher real income (odds ratio 2.21) and working at a large firm (1.64) increased the likelihood of leaving.
  • In contrast, day laborers (0.57), homeowners (0.73), and older people with longer job tenure were more likely to stay. Pre-move income was also higher for every migration type than for non-movers, contrary to earlier findings that low-income people leave.

Binary logit results estimating out-migration (reference: non-movers) from characteristics in 2015 (*** p<0.001). Values above 1 mean a higher likelihood of leaving; income and large-firm employment raised out-migration most strongly.

  • In the multinomial logit, the high-income and large-firm effects appeared for every destination. The large-firm effect was largest for the Seoul metropolitan area (2.08) and non-capital small and medium cities (2.13). Competitive people did not necessarily choose the capital region.
  • What determined the income change rate was the kind of job people got, more than where they went. The standardized coefficients of job tenure, employment status, and firm size were larger than those of destination, and among destinations only the Seoul metropolitan area (0.034) had a positive effect.
  • There was no effect in the year of migration (−1.1%p). But from 1 year later, migrants’ income change rate was 5.4%p higher than that of stayers, and a gap of about 2.8–2.9%p persisted after 2–3 years.
  • In the unbalanced panel up to 6 years later, the gap was 2.7%p after 1 year and then narrowed to 1.1–1.4%p, but it was significant at every time point. The gap is not temporary; it accumulates.

  • By group, the post-migration effect on the income change rate was larger for women than for men, and for people from small businesses than for people from large firms.

Implications

  • Depopulation should be reframed not as “young people leave, older people stay” but as an outflow of high-quality human capital and employment capacity.
  • Employment characteristics, not region, drove income. Expanding quality jobs through support for conversion to regular employment, incentives for long-term tenure, and attracting firms and public institutions could help ease out-migration.
  • The findings can support tailored policies that move beyond uniform Local Extinction Response Fund projects, for example by splitting support into short- and mid-to-long-term phases that match the pace of post-migration income change.

Limitations & Next Steps

  • The data lacked human-capital variables such as education, educational attainment, and family composition, so migration factors could not be fully controlled. Individual fixed effects were also not controlled.
  • The observation period for each migration cohort is at most 6 years, short relative to the average length of residence (about 7.9 years).
  • Job changes were not identified separately; identifying them to show changes in employment characteristics more clearly is left for future research.

Commissioned Research 2

A Study on Establishing a Korean Water Culture

Commissioned Research · K-water Operation & Management Co. · 2024.9 – 2025.1

Client K-water Operation & Management Co.
Conducted by Seoul National University R&DB Foundation
Period 2024.9 – 2025.1
Role Project manager · research assistant (research administration, literature review, survey design and analysis, report writing)

Background

  • The Framework Act on Water Management states the need for, and support of, fostering water culture, but it does not define what water culture is. As a result, each institution and policy interpreted it differently.
  • Water management once aimed at flood control and water supply; more recently, there is a stronger trend to also consider the social, artistic, and experiential value of water.
  • Abroad, starting with Spain’s New Water Culture movement in 1995, the EU, UNESCO, and others have emphasized establishing a water culture grounded in the multidimensional value of water.

Research Questions

  • What elements make up a Korean water culture, and how can it be defined?
  • How well do citizens know water culture, and which values of water do they consider important?
  • What should K-water Operation & Management Co., which operates water culture hub facilities, do, and in what order?

Data & Methods

  • I analyzed domestic and international literature to derive the pillars of water culture and define the concept.
  • I designed a nationwide online survey of 400 people (2024.12.9–16) on awareness of water culture, the value of water, use of water culture centers, and attitudes toward protecting water, and ran frequency and cross-tabulation analyses.
  • Drawing on international cases such as the OECD, I mapped domestic water culture stakeholders.
  • I conducted field surveys at the Chungju Dam and Seomjingang Dam water culture centers and the Geumgang River culture center, and reviewed domestic and international cases of spreading water culture.
  • Combining these results, I carried out a SWOT analysis and developed a vision, strategies, and an institutional roadmap.

Key Findings

  • I defined water culture along four pillars: history and tradition, society and economy, environment and ecology, and arts and leisure. All values and ways of life created through the interaction of water and people from these four perspectives count as water culture.
  • Only 77 of 400 respondents (19.3%) had heard the term “water culture.” They mostly encountered it on the internet (39.0%), TV and news media (28.6%), and social media (19.5%).
  • By contrast, 92.5% said efforts to preserve and develop water culture are important. This revealed a gap: the term is unfamiliar, but people share its values.
  • As important values of water (multiple responses), respondents chose environment and ecology (97.5%) and society and economy (83.5%), while history and tradition (9.0%) and leisure and arts (10.0%) were low. Respondents in their 30s chose leisure and arts relatively more often, at 17.8%.
  • The SWOT analysis identified rich water heritage and stable water management infrastructure as strengths, and low public awareness, the lack of a control tower, and a shortage of specialists as weaknesses.
  • I set the vision as “A sustainable water-friendly nation built on water culture” and proposed 3 goals (raising water awareness and managing demand, fostering a rich water culture, and sustainable water use) and 4 strategies (space management, stronger networks, content development, and industry development).
  • Treating K-water Operation & Management Co. as the dedicated water culture institution, I organized the tasks for each of the 4 strategies into a short-, mid-, and long-term roadmap.

Implications

  • Public campaigns and promotion (30.0%) and stronger government policy (26.7%) were the most frequently chosen means of spreading water culture. The public sector needs to take an active role in civic education and promotion.
  • Water culture centers and river culture centers are key hubs of water culture but lacked networking, specialists, and educational programs. They need to become cultural hubs connected with local communities.
  • To broaden perceptions that lean toward environment and socio-economics, content on history and tradition and on arts and leisure should be planned as well.

Revising the Guidelines for Comprehensive Water Demand Management Plans

Commissioned Research · Korea Water Resources Corporation (K-water) · 2023.12 – 2024.8

Client Korea Water Resources Corporation (K-water)
Conducted by Seoul National University R&DB Foundation
Period 2023.12 – 2024.8
Role Research assistant (literature review of domestic and international systems, proposal and report writing)

Background

  • Starting with the Comprehensive Water Conservation Measures in 2000, Korea’s 17 provinces and metropolitan cities now prepare and implement the 4th Comprehensive Water Demand Management Plan (2021–2025).
  • But each province set targets and calculated savings differently, making it hard to evaluate target achievement or compare performance across regions.
  • Savings were also miscalculated in some cases. For sewage reuse, for example, plans and results were calculated differently, producing unrealistically high achievement rates.
  • With more frequent droughts and extreme floods under climate change, systematically reducing demand has become more important than expanding supply.

Research Questions

  • How should the guidelines change so that provinces set water-saving targets and evaluate results on the same basis?
  • How can measures whose savings are hard to calculate, such as education and promotion, be included in planning and evaluation?

Data & Methods

  • I summarized how Korea’s water demand management system has evolved and how it relates to other statutory plans.
  • I analyzed how each province prepared and implemented the 3rd comprehensive plan, along with its evaluation system, to diagnose problems.
  • I reviewed water demand management systems and guidelines in the United States, Australia, the United Kingdom, Singapore, and elsewhere, and drew lessons for Korea’s guidelines.
  • Based on this, we produced a revised draft of the 5th-plan guidelines and a standard plan template (example), with improved target-setting criteria, performance evaluation methods, and policy measures.

Key Findings

  • Other countries also rarely provided standardized target formulas at the local-government level. Instead, the biggest difference from Korea was that they actively use economic incentives alongside regulation.

Table 1. Key features of international water demand management systems

Country Key features
United States Planning support tool reflecting local conditions (BMP Matrix), WaterSense product rebates, block and seasonal rates
Australia Water efficiency labeling (WELS) and rebates for high-efficiency products, mandatory water efficiency plans for large water users
United Kingdom Under the Environment Act (2021), a 20% cut in per capita public water supply use by 2037-38; 5-year WRMPs prepared by each water company
Singapore ‘Target 130’ sets a goal of 130 L per person per day by 2030; water conservation tax; 10% savings challenge for businesses

A summary of the country reviews in Chapter 2 of the report. All four countries share Korea's broad framework of supply, use, and reuse, but their incentives, such as rates and rebates, and their management of large water users were more specific.

  • We restructured demand management into four stages by adding a “water culture improvement stage” to supply, reuse, and use. Quantitative measures whose savings can be calculated and qualitative measures such as education and promotion are handled separately.
  • We provided unified savings formulas for each stage. The supply stage uses the revenue water ratio of the previous plan period and the current year, the reuse stage uses facility utilization rates and rainfall analysis, and the use stage uses the water-saving grade of water-saving fixtures.
  • We specified criteria for linking with related plans. The supply stage gives priority to the Water Supply Improvement Plan and the reuse stage to each province’s Water Reuse Management Plan, while reflecting changing conditions.
  • Provinces are to set water rate rationalization targets suited to local conditions and finances, and to prepare economic incentives.
  • We provided example standardized performance evaluation weights for provinces to use.

Table 2. Performance evaluation weights (example)

Stage Evaluation item Weight
Supply stage Revenue water ratio / leakage rate management · per capita daily use 30% · 10%
Use stage Water-saving fixtures 15%
Reuse stage Rainwater use / graywater systems / treated wastewater reuse 15%
Water culture improvement stage Water rate rationalization · education and promotion, WASCO program promotion, etc. 15% · 15%

Example evaluation weights included in the revised guidelines (Table 67 in the original report). The supply stage carries the highest weight, while the new water culture improvement stage also counts for 30% in total. Provinces must adjust the weights to local conditions and justify them.

Implications

  • Unifying formulas and evaluation methods makes targets and performance more reliable and enables comparisons across provinces.
  • Given population decline, targets should go beyond cutting per capita use to efficient water management across the whole process, from supply to use.
  • Improving water culture is a long-term means of changing people’s behavior. This discussion later led to the Korean water culture study.

Limitations & Next Steps

  • Current water data are supply-based, making it hard to understand demand shifts driven by changes such as the rise of single-person households or the semiconductor industry. End-use data by purpose, facility, and population characteristics need to be built.
  • To prepare for climate change, water demand management plans for droughts, storm and flood damage, and emergencies need to be more specific.

Research Projects 5

Effects of Firm Size and Regional Characteristics on Innovation Output

Team Project · Urban & Regional Analysis · 2024.9 – 2024.12

Type Course team project (3 members)
Role Team leader · proposed the topic, reviewed prior studies, preprocessed and compiled data, interpreted results
Data Patent applications and knowledge-based industry establishment data for 225 si-gun-gu nationwide (2022)
Methods OLS, geographically weighted regression (GWR)

Background

  • In 2012, large firms filed more patent applications than SMEs, but in 2022, SMEs’ applications surpassed those of large and mid-sized firms.
  • As innovation has flourished in urban clusters such as Pangyo and Magok, there has been growing discussion that small firms can also lead innovation where inter-firm collaboration and labor sharing are easy.
  • Conversely, in regions where linking with nearby firms is difficult, large firms with strong in-house capabilities may lead innovation. I focused on the possibility that the relationship between firm size and innovation differs by region.

Research Questions

  • In knowledge-based industries, do larger establishments lead to more patent applications?
  • How does that effect differ by region, such as between the Seoul metropolitan area and the rest of the country?

Data & Methods

  • We analyzed 225 si-gun-gu nationwide in 2022. Island areas far from other regions (Ongjin-gun, Ulleung-gun, Jeju-si, Seogwipo-si) were excluded as unsuitable for GWR.
  • The dependent variable was the number of patent applications by si-gun-gu (natural log) from the Intellectual Property Statistics Service (IPSS). Instead of patent registrations, which take nearly 2 years, we used applications, which reflect the most recent knowledge-creation activity.
  • The key variables were the average establishment size (employees ÷ establishments) in knowledge-based services (professional, scientific, and technical services) and knowledge-based manufacturing (13 industries with a patent application share of 1% or more).
  • By also controlling for employment in both industries, we designed the establishment size variable to mean “whether the same workforce is concentrated in a few establishments or spread across many.” Population, population density, commercial-zone area, and the number of graduate students were also controlled.
  • We estimated the nationwide average effect with heteroskedasticity-robust OLS and region-specific effects with geographically weighted regression (GWR). Because administrative areas vary greatly in size, we used an adaptive bandwidth chosen to minimize the corrected AIC.

Key Findings

  • Patent applications averaged 811, but the gap was very large, from a minimum of 4 to a maximum of 15,645 (Suwon-si). Applications were concentrated in the Seoul metropolitan area, Cheonan, and Asan, and in manufacturing cities such as Ulsan, Pohang, Gumi, and Changwon.
  • In the OLS results, each 1-person increase in average establishment size in knowledge-based services raised patent applications by about 4.9%. In contrast, establishment size in knowledge-based manufacturing was not significant.
  • Each additional 1,000 employees raised patent applications by about 1.9% in services and about 1.0% in manufacturing. An increase of 1,000 graduate students was associated with an increase of about 7.4%, confirming the importance of higher education institutions.

  • In the GWR, the effect of services establishment size ranged from about 1.2% to 11.5% depending on the region. It was positive everywhere, but its magnitude varied widely.
  • In the Seoul metropolitan area, including Seoul, the coefficients were low and not statistically significant. The size effect grew toward the southern coast, especially toward Gyeongsangnam-do.
  • Manufacturing establishment size was not significant in most regions. It was, however, significantly positive in parts of Chungcheongnam-do and Jeollabuk-do, such as Daejeon and Jeonju, and in Inje, Yangyang, Pyeongchang, and Hongcheon in Gangwon.

Implications

  • The results show that in the Seoul metropolitan area, with its dense population and firms and an established regional innovation system, innovation can occur regardless of establishment size.
  • This is consistent with prior research showing that collaborating small firms can narrow the innovation gap with large firms (Nieto & Santamaría, 2010). Support for SMEs may matter more in large cities with strong innovation environments.
  • In contrast, in non-capital regions such as Yeongnam, larger establishments have an advantage in innovation, so innovation policy should be designed to fit regional characteristics.

Limitations & Next Steps

  • The si-gun-gu unit is too large to capture recent, densely formed industrial clusters. Even within Yongin-si, for example, residential Gwanggyo and Giheung-gu, home to the semiconductor industry, differ greatly in innovation characteristics.
  • This was not possible this time because of patent data limitations, but analysis with patent application data at the administrative-dong level could yield more insights.

Factors Behind Changes in Bus Boardings and Alightings after New Public Transit Policies

BK Interdisciplinary Seminar · 2024.4 – 2024.8

Type BK Interdisciplinary Seminar joint research (5 members, final presentation 2024.8.26)
Role Team leader · research administration, literature review, data preprocessing and analysis, presentation
Data Bus boardings and alightings for 426 administrative dongs in Seoul · building floor area by use
Methods T-test, Moran's I, spatial error model (SEM)

Background

  • Traffic congestion, air pollution, and energy consumption are growing problems. This makes TOD (transit-oriented development), which builds cities around public transit rather than private cars, increasingly important.
  • In the first half of 2024, Seoul’s Climate Card (pilot in January) and K-Pass (May, replacing the Altteul Transportation Card) were introduced in quick succession. K-Pass passed 2 million users within 3 months of launch.
  • These new policies called for empirical evidence on where actual transit use changed, and with which factors.

Research Questions

  • After the new transit policies, did bus boardings and alightings in Seoul change differently than before?
  • Which building uses, measured as floor area by use in each administrative dong, are associated with bus use?

Data & Methods

  • Using Seoul’s 426 administrative dongs as the unit, I used bus boardings and alightings from the Public Data Portal (February–June, 2023–2024) as the dependent variable.
  • The independent variables are building floor area by use (2024) from the Building Data Open System. Following prior research (Kim Ji-yun et al., 2015), I grouped them into major categories such as residential, commercial, office, and cultural & assembly, and subcategories such as apartment housing and Class 1 neighborhood living facilities.
  • As a pilot test, I compared monthly changes in boardings and alightings before (2018→2019) and after (2023→2024) the policies with a T-test, and checked spatial autocorrelation with Moran’s I.
  • Based on the spatial autocorrelation of residuals and Lagrange multiplier (LM) tests, I chose a spatial error model (SEM) over OLS and the spatial lag model. I estimated a base model with major-category variables and an extended model with subcategory variables.

Key Findings

  • The T-test showed that in every month from February to June, changes in bus boardings and alightings differed significantly before and after the policies (t = -22.66 to -26.86, p < 0.001).
  • Moran’s I for the changes was 0.156–0.200 in 2018–2019 and 0.147–0.175 in 2023–2024. This indicates modest but positive spatial autocorrelation: when use changes in one neighborhood, nearby neighborhoods tend to change similarly.
  • In the spatial dependence diagnostics, Moran’s I of the OLS residuals was 0.159 (p < 0.001), violating OLS assumptions. Robust LM (lag) was not significant (p = 0.339) and only Robust LM (error) was significant (p < 0.001), so SEM was appropriate for all months from February to June.

  • In the base model, residential, cultural, commercial, and office floor area were significant positive factors in all months from February to June. In May, the remaining uses, such as education & welfare and parks & industry, were not significant at the 5% level.
  • In the extended model, apartment housing, office facilities, Class 1 neighborhood living facilities, retail facilities, cultural & assembly facilities, and religious facilities were significant across February to June. All variables had VIFs below 10, so multicollinearity was not a concern.
  • I interpreted commercial areas as drawing bus use because they are centers of daily activity with good walkability and dense bus routes. Religious facilities often have little parking and sit in residential or mixed-use areas, which I saw as translating into transit demand.

Implications

  • By comparing changes in use before and after the new transit policies at the administrative-dong level, I provided empirical evidence for gauging policy effects.
  • By identifying the specific building uses associated with bus use, the study offers variables to consider when designing future TOD policies.
  • The dong-level results can serve as baseline data for locally tailored transit policies.

Limitations & Next Steps

  • Usage data for the Climate Card were not public, so I could not directly isolate the effect of a specific policy.
  • Non-policy factors such as weather and economic conditions were not fully accounted for.
  • Transit use varies by time of day and by weekday versus weekend, so finer time segmentation would yield more realistic results.

Green Infrastructure as a Catalyst for Urban Regeneration

Joint research · Cheonggyecheon restoration and private development

Type Joint research (4 members, co-author)
Role Data collection and preprocessing, report writing
Study area Parcels around the restored section of Cheonggyecheon Stream (1993–2015)
Data 79,830 observations, including building permits, officially assessed individual land prices, and zoning
Methods Logistic DID, K-means cluster analysis

Background

  • Green infrastructure is drawing attention as a key tool for urban regeneration because of its varied economic, social, and environmental benefits.
  • Existing studies have mainly shown that green infrastructure raises nearby land prices or commercial sales. However, they have not fully explained its long-term effects or its role in attracting private development.
  • The Cheonggyecheon restoration turned an aging elevated highway, a piece of gray infrastructure, into a walkable waterfront green space. It is a landmark case of reviving the declining commercial core of Seoul’s historic downtown.

Research Questions

  • How much does public green infrastructure stimulate private development in surrounding areas?
  • How does this effect vary with parcel characteristics (use, land price, accessibility)?

Data & Methods

  • Of the roughly 11 km of Cheonggyecheon, I chose the roughly 1.7 km urban-stream section crossing downtown and its surrounding parcels as the study area. I excluded the cultural/natural-stream section, which is widely mixed with markets, and the residential/everyday-stream section.
  • I compiled building permit data from the National Spatial Data Infrastructure Portal (NSDI) at the parcel level. New construction, reconstruction, and change of use counted as private development; balcony alterations and temporary structures were excluded.
  • I compared the 10 years before (1993–2002) and after (2006–2015) the project, and estimated a logistic difference-in-differences (DID) model with the occurrence of private development (0/1) as the dependent variable. There are 79,830 observations.
  • The net effect was measured with a “post-project period × distance to Cheonggyecheon” interaction term. I controlled for parcel size, officially assessed individual land price, zoning, number of subway lines within 300 m, and whether height restrictions had been lifted.
  • I ran a K-means cluster analysis (K=5) on parcel size, zoning, assessed land price, and subway accessibility, then estimated the DID model separately for each cluster.

Key Findings

  • In 5-year periods, the share of parcels with private development was 0.51% in Phase I and 2.54% in Phase II, then rose sharply to 5.69% in Phase III, right after the restoration. It remained higher than before the project in Phase IV, at 4.69%.

An interaction odds ratio below 1 means that after the project, the probability of development declined with distance from Cheonggyecheon. In other words, parcels closer to the stream saw more private development. *** p<0.001

  • The interaction term was significant in both models: after the restoration, parcels closer to the stream were more likely to see private development.
  • Larger parcels, higher land prices, and better subway access were associated with higher development probability. Central commercial zones were about 2.38 times more likely than Class 1 residential zones.
  • Contrary to expectations, areas where height restrictions were lifted had lower development probability. I interpreted this as many of these areas lying in the Sewoon District, where long-delayed redevelopment blocked individual projects.
  • The cluster analysis identified five types: residential, semi-peripheral, central commercial, office, and peripheral. Development increased after the project in all types, but the distance effect was significant only for “central commercial” (0.996*) and “office” (0.998).

Implications

  • The study provides empirical evidence that public green infrastructure attracts private development and that the effect lasts for nearly 10 years.
  • The effect was strongest on high-value commercial and office parcels, consistent with the Seoul Metropolitan Government’s aim of shifting downtown toward high-value-added industries.
  • It shows that even cities with limited budgets can use green infrastructure as seed investment for regeneration strategies. However, institutions that block individual development, such as redevelopment zone designations, can offset the effect and should also be considered.

Contributing to Carbon Neutrality through Mobility Transition

Course research report · Smart Cities and Social Innovation · 2024.3 – 2024.6

Type Course research report
Role Literature review, analysis of domestic and international policy and city cases, report writing and presentation
Methods Comparative case analysis from technology, institutional, and civic-participation perspectives

Background

  • Korea’s 2050 Carbon Neutrality Scenario set a target of cutting transport emissions by 37.8% from 2018 levels to 61.0 million tons, relying mainly on the spread of electric and hydrogen vehicles.
  • Since late 2023, however, the EV market has entered a temporary demand slowdown, the so-called “chasm.” Price gaps, short driving range, and long charging times are cited as causes.
  • With a transition that relied on consumers replacing their cars stalling, policies were needed to drive the mobility transition at the public level rather than the individual level.

Research Questions

  • Beyond changing cars, how can a modal shift to public transit, cycling, and walking contribute to carbon neutrality?
  • Compared with leading cities abroad, what is missing from Korea’s policies?

Cases & Methods

  • I reviewed domestic and international literature, including the national carbon neutrality plan, OECD transport strategy reports, and the Jeju Smart City Plan.
  • I organized Zurich, Switzerland, and Copenhagen, Denmark, from three perspectives (technological innovation, policy and institutions, and civic participation) and compared Korean policies using the same framework.
  • I analyzed transit-shift tools in three groups: fare-free public transit (Tallinn), unlimited-ride passes (Germany’s 9-Euro Ticket, Seoul’s Climate Card), and fare incentives (K-Pass).

Key Findings

  • Zurich restricts private cars by reducing parking and limiting more than 50% of its roads to 30 km/h, while giving public transit signal priority. Only 37% of residents own a car.
  • Copenhagen aims for 75% of all trips by walking, cycling, and public transit, and has more than 500 km of cycling infrastructure. Since 1970, downtown cycling has risen by about 137% and car use has fallen by 32%.
  • Korean policies center on R&D and green-vehicle subsidies, so compared with these cities, their private-car restrictions and civic-participation mechanisms were relatively weak.
  • After Tallinn introduced fare-free transit, the bus mode share rose from 55% to 63% and the car share fell from 31% to 28%. The 9-Euro Ticket increased ridership by 25% over 3 months and cut greenhouse gas emissions by 1.8 million tons.
  • However, I also found counterarguments that the added riders came mainly from walking and cycling, with little shift from car users.

Implications

  • Fare cuts alone are not enough. Regulations and incentives must be designed together, such as higher parking fees and congestion charges, to shift car users.
  • Technology investment should also go beyond green vehicles to technologies that benefit more people, such as intelligent transport systems, smart pedestrian infrastructure, and shared mobility.
  • A “just transition” accessible to everyone, including transportation-disadvantaged groups, is needed, along with ways to involve citizens as stakeholders, such as data sharing.

Limitations & Next Steps

  • Most public transit operations in Korea run deficits, so the fiscal sustainability of fare-cut policies needs separate review.
  • The lack of incentives targeting people in their 40s and 50s, who have high car ownership, remains a task for future work.

Sustainable Regional Innovation through Vacant House Reuse

Course research report · 2023.9 – 2023.12

Type Course research report
Role Literature review, domestic and international case research and analysis, report writing and presentation
Methods Comparative case analysis from an ESG perspective (6 domestic and international cases)

Background

  • Low fertility and population aging are reshaping the population structure, and as industries move to cities, local young populations are shrinking.
  • Aging houses are concentrated in poor housing environments such as hillside areas and semi-basements, and the number of vacant houses grows every year.
  • Vacant houses lead to blighted landscapes, safety problems, and population decline, and the problem is more severe in more aged areas.

Research Questions

  • What do cases that revived abandoned vacant houses as local assets have in common?
  • How do appropriate technology and collaboration among diverse actors contribute to the sustainability of vacant house reuse?

Cases & Methods

  • I reviewed 9 prior studies on the concepts of vacant houses, appropriate technology, and ESG, and on the use of idle spaces, and reinterpreted them from an ESG perspective.
  • I examined 6 domestic and international cases: Dazayo in Jeju, Seogyo Art Experiment Center, the Onomichi Vacant House Renewal Project, the Yanaka historic district in Taito City, the Westway Trust in the UK, and the Incheon Smart Urban Farmer Cooperative.
  • For each case, I compared the operating model (Type), Actor, Activity, Resource, and Sustainability.

Key Findings

  • Dazayo in Jeju leases vacant houses free of charge for 10 years, renovates them into lodging, recovers costs through rental income, and then returns them to the original owners. Part of the revenue goes to a village development fund.
  • In Onomichi and Yanaka, resident-led NPOs collected information on vacant houses and converted them into cafés, guesthouses, and share houses.
  • The Westway Trust transferred operation from the local government to a social enterprise and worked with about 60 resident groups to run the space under an elevated highway as a community sports and gathering space.
  • The Smart Urban Farmer Cooperative grows mushrooms in vacant semi-basement homes, creating jobs for vulnerable groups such as seniors and women with career breaks.
  • The lasting cases shared resident participation, public-private governance, and revenue structures that do not depend solely on public support.

Table 1. Comparison of key cases (4 of 6)

Case Type Operator Resources & sustainability
Dazayo, Jeju B2B → B2C Company Free lease and crowdfunding; part of revenue donated
Onomichi Vacant House Renewal G2C, B2C Public-private governance Local government subsidies and corporate support; close ties with residents
Westway Trust, UK G2C → B2C Local government → social enterprise Own revenue from subsidies + external resources + links with local groups
Smart Urban Farmer Cooperative G2C Public agency, public-private governance Free or half-price leases under an LH agreement; minimal public support

The original report compared 6 cases by Type, Actor, Activity, Resource, and Sustainability; here I selected the 4 with clearly different operating models. Even when the public sector provides the space, cases tend to last longer as operation and revenue shift to residents and the private sector.

Implications

  • Environment: Vacant houses should be seen as assets that hold local history and culture, and appropriate technology should be used to find uses suited to local conditions.
  • Social: Residents should participate directly, and opportunities for community participation and jobs should be expanded for vulnerable groups.
  • Governance: Long-term operation requires a clear operating entity and a system for coordinating stakeholder views.

Limitations & Next Steps

  • Because I redefined ESG at the spatial level as “a strategy for revitalizing the local economy through environmental conservation and stronger social solidarity,” direct comparison with existing ESG research was difficult.
  • The scope of the case research and legal constraints also remain limitations.
